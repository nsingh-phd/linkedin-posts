The Two-Thirds Door

Three doors. One car. You pick, the host opens a goat, and offers you a swap. Almost everyone says it’s a coin flip now. Switching wins twice as often. Here is why — starting with you playing it.

01

Your first pick is frozen at one third

When you chose, the car was already placed and you knew nothing. Your door had a 1 in 3 chance. The other two doors held the remaining 2 in 3 between them.

Then the host opens a door — but only ever a goat door, and never yours. That reveal tells you nothing new about your door, so its third doesn’t move. What it does tell you is exactly where the other two thirds went: all of it piles onto the one door left standing.

Three worlds — that is the entire proof

03

Switching just inverts your first guess

Here is the shortest way to hold it in your head. Switching doesn’t give you a fresh chance — it flips the verdict on the guess you already made. Guessed right? Switching loses. Guessed wrong? Switching wins. So the only question worth asking is the easy one:

How often is a blind 1-in-3 guess wrong?

06

It only works because the host knows

The two thirds are not conjured out of the doors. They are handed to you by the host, and the proof is what happens when you take his knowledge away. Let him open a door at random instead. Throw out the rounds where he spoils it by revealing the car, and look only at the rounds that still reach the offer — rounds that look, to you, identical to the real game.

The host knows

He skips the car every time. Surviving a reveal tells you nothing about your door, so its 1/3 stands and the rest concentrates.

The host is guessing

He opens a goat only by luck. Reaching the offer at all is now evidence — and it favours your door just as much as the other one.

Same doors, same reveal, same offer. The only thing that changed is what the host knew when he chose which door to open — and that is where your extra third came from all along.

You are not betting on a door. You are betting that your first guess was wrong.
